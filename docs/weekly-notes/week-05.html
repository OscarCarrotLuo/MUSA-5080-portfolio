Week 5: Regression, Prediction, and Model Evaluation

How a regression learns a line, what residuals mean, and how to evaluate predictions with honest cross-validation.
Published

September 25, 2026

What We Did

We used Philadelphia Census tracts to ask where monthly rents are higher than a model would predict from neighborhood characteristics. The lesson connected linear regression, least squares, coefficient interpretation, survey uncertainty, train/test evaluation, cross-validation, and residual maps.

These notes combine my original class notes and board photographs with the Week 5 lab and a peer’s topic checklist. Numerical examples explicitly marked “board example” are teaching values, not newly estimated Philadelphia results.

Key Concepts

  • Prediction asks for an accurate \(\hat y\); coefficient inference asks what we can learn about \(\beta\). Neither automatically establishes causation.
  • A residual is observed minus fitted: \(e_i=y_i-\hat y_i\). Positive means rent is higher than predicted; negative means lower.
  • Ordinary least squares minimizes squared vertical residuals.
  • A good training fit does not guarantee accurate predictions on new tracts.
  • Evaluate candidate models using the same rows, folds, outcome units, and metric.
  • Survey coefficient of variation and model cross-validation both abbreviate to CV, but answer different questions.

Notes

1. The modeling table and the question

Each row represents a tract, not a household. The target is a tract-level median monthly gross rent. Income is a tract-level median annual household income.

Variable Role and meaning Unit or check
GEOID Tract identifier Character text; preserve leading zeros
med_rentE Outcome: estimated median gross rent Dollars per month
med_incE Predictor: estimated median household income Dollars per year
pct_renter Renter-occupied / all occupied housing units Lab uses 0–100, not 0–1
pct_ba BA or higher / population age 25+ Lab uses 0–100
n_stations Number of nearby Indego bike-share stations Count, not a causal treatment
rent_cv Relative sampling uncertainty of the rent estimate Percent
geometry Tract polygon Keep for spatial calculations and mapping

The handwritten 0.71 corresponds to a proportion of 71%; the lab’s percentage variable would be 71. With a 0–100 predictor, increasing it by 1 means one percentage point. With a 0–1 proportion, one percentage point is 0.01. The renter denominator is occupied housing units, not all residents.

The lab counts stations within half a mile of the tract polygon. A station inside a tract has distance zero; one near any part of its boundary may count. This does not establish that every resident is within a half-mile walk.

tracts <- philly_tracts |>
  mutate(
    pct_renter = 100 * renter_occE / occ_unitsE,
    pct_ba = 100 * (baE + maE + profE + phdE) / edu_totalE,
    rent_se = med_rentM / 1.645,
    rent_cv = 100 * rent_se / med_rentE
  )

Before fitting, check positive denominators and finite values. Build a common analysis sample for all candidate models, report which rows were lost, and keep GEOIDs in the same order. Otherwise lm() may omit rows and residuals may no longer line up with the map.

2. The unknown relationship, fitted values, and errors

The conceptual data-generating relationship is

\[ Y=f(X)+\epsilon. \]

Here \(f\) is an unknown relationship, \(X\) is the available information, and \(\epsilon\) is remaining variation. A simple linear model approximates it by

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+\epsilon_i, \qquad \hat Y_i=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1X_i. \]

The prediction formula has no added unknown \(\epsilon_i\): we cannot know that individual error in advance. The theoretical error \(\epsilon_i\) and fitted residual \(e_i\) are related but distinct; residuals become observable after fitting.

Objective Example question Evidence to examine
Prediction, \(\hat y\) What monthly rent should we expect for a tract? Held-out errors and stability
Inference, \(\hat\beta\) How much does predicted rent differ with income, conditional on included predictors? Coefficients, uncertainty, assumptions
Causal inference Would installing a station change rent? A credible causal design, beyond an observational coefficient

An Indego-station count can help prediction by representing centrality or amenities. That predictive usefulness does not prove that stations cause rents to increase. Tract relationships also do not establish individual household relationships: that would risk an ecological inference error.

3. Flexibility and model families

The board placed linear models at the simpler end and trees, forests, boosting, and neural networks toward the more flexible end. Treat this as an intuition, not a universal ranking. Flexibility depends on features and tuning: a shallow tree may be simple, while a linear regression with many basis functions may be flexible.

  • Linear regression specifies a form and estimates its coefficients.
  • Ridge and lasso regularize coefficients; with the same features, their penalties generally constrain flexibility rather than automatically increasing it.
  • Logistic regression models probabilities for a categorical outcome. It is not simply the next more flexible model for predicting a continuous rent amount.
  • Nonparametric methods allow the fitted relationship’s complexity to adapt more to the data. “Nonparametric” does not mean that there are no parameters or assumptions.

Greater flexibility can reduce underfitting but may fit accidental patterns in the training sample. Validation tells us whether the extra complexity helps.

4. How least squares chooses the line

For every observation, compute a residual and square it:

\[ e_i=y_i-\hat y_i, \qquad RSS=\sum_i e_i^2. \]

OLS chooses coefficients that minimize \(RSS\). Squaring stops positive and negative residuals from canceling and gives larger misses greater weight. The distances are vertical, in the outcome direction; OLS does not minimize the shortest perpendicular distance to the line.

5. Reading lm() output in meaningful units

m1 <- lm(med_rentE ~ med_incE, data = tracts)
summary(m1)
coef(m1)["med_incE"] * 10000

The rounded board example gives this fitted equation:

\[ \widehat{\text{monthly rent}}=690+0.0081\times\text{annual income}. \]

An income difference of $10,000 corresponds to an $81 difference in predicted monthly rent. At income $82,000, predicted rent is $1,354.20. If observed rent is $1,650, the residual is $295.80: observed rent exceeds the prediction.

The intercept is the prediction at income zero. This can be outside the data’s useful range, so it is not necessarily a meaningful description of actual tracts.

Output Interpretation
Estimate Fitted coefficient, with units determined by \(Y\) and \(X\)
Standard error Estimated uncertainty of the coefficient, not a tract’s prediction error
t statistic (estimate - null value) / SE; the usual null value is zero
p-value Under the null and assumptions, probability of a statistic at least this extreme

For the board slope and rounded SE, \(0.0081/0.0005=16.2\). A two-sided 5% test uses a t critical value based on residual degrees of freedom; 1.96 is a large- sample approximation. A small p-value does not measure predictive accuracy, practical importance, or the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

In multiple regression, a slope describes an association holding the other included predictors constant. Changing the model can change that slope.

6. Noise in the outcome and in predictors

Random outcome measurement error that is mean-zero conditional on the predictors adds uncertainty and tends to enlarge residuals; it does not by itself create a systematic slope bias. Outcome errors related to predictors can bias results. Residuals may also contain omitted systematic patterns, model misspecification, and spatial dependence, not just random noise.

Classical independent measurement error in the single predictor of a simple regression tends to shrink its slope toward zero: attenuation bias. In multiple regression or with nonclassical measurement error, the direction is not guaranteed. The board’s “noise in X flattens the slope” needs these conditions.

ACS median rent is itself an estimate. Its 90% margin of error can be converted approximately to a standard error by dividing by 1.645. For a positive estimate,

\[ CV_{\text{estimate}}=100\times\frac{SE}{\text{estimate}}. \]

A 35% CV means the SE is 35% of the estimate; the 90% MOE is approximately 57.6% of it. It does not tell us exactly what fraction of a model residual is sampling noise. Large residuals should be considered alongside estimate uncertainty before being treated as policy flags.

7. Training fit versus prediction performance

For an ordinary unweighted model with an intercept, training

\[ R^2=1-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}. \]

Adding predictors on the same training rows cannot decrease ordinary training \(R^2\), even if the additions do not generalize. Adjusted \(R^2\) penalizes complexity but still does not replace held-out evaluation. \(R^2\) can also be calculated out of sample, where it can be negative; always state which data produced a metric.

RMSE takes the root of the average squared prediction error:

\[ RMSE=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}. \]

For observed rents (500, 900) and predictions (400, 1200), the errors are (100, -300), squared errors are (10000, 90000), and RMSE is about $223.61. RMSE has the same units as rent and emphasizes large errors. It is not the mean absolute error (which is $200 in this example).

summary(lm)$sigma uses residual degrees of freedom in its denominator, whereas training RMSE uses \(n\). They need not match.

8. Train/test splits and K-fold cross-validation

An 80/20 split fits a model on 80% of observations and evaluates predictions on the unused 20%. Training error is usually smaller, though an individual random split can be an exception. A single split can give a lucky or unlucky result.

Five-fold CV partitions 100 records into five groups of 20. Fit five separate models, each on 80 records, and predict the remaining 20. Every observation gets one out-of-fold prediction from a model that did not train on that observation.

Two legitimate summaries are often both called “CV RMSE”:

\[ \text{mean fold RMSE}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_k RMSE_k, \]

\[ \text{pooled OOF RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_k n_k RMSE_k^2}{\sum_k n_k}}. \]

They generally differ because taking a square root and taking an average do not commute. The Week 6 helper uses pooled out-of-fold RMSE; the lab’s caret output typically reports the mean resampled RMSE. Use one definition consistently.

The following base-R pattern makes the common folds explicit. It assumes a previously prepared complete model_df, with one row per tract.

model_df <- sf::st_drop_geometry(tracts)
set.seed(5080)
fold_id <- sample(rep(1:5, length.out = nrow(model_df)))
oof <- rep(NA_real_, nrow(model_df))

for (k in sort(unique(fold_id))) {
  holdout <- fold_id == k
  fit <- lm(med_rentE ~ med_incE + pct_renter + pct_ba + n_stations,
            data = model_df[!holdout, ], na.action = na.fail)
  oof[holdout] <- predict(fit, newdata = model_df[holdout, ])
}
stopifnot(all(is.finite(oof)))
sqrt(mean((model_df$med_rentE - oof)^2))

9. Choosing features without leaking information

Compare candidate feature sets on identical rows and folds. A variable with a small p-value need not improve prediction, and a useful predictor need not have a stable causal interpretation. Prefer an improvement that is repeatable and large enough to matter, rather than overinterpreting a tiny RMSE difference.

Imputation medians, scaling parameters, data-driven cutoffs, and feature selection must be learned from the training portion of each split. Otherwise information from validation observations leaks into model construction. Reusing CV scores to choose among many models also makes the winner’s reported score optimistic; an untouched final test set or nested CV provides a more independent assessment.

Randomly split neighboring tracts may be very similar. If deployment means predicting new neighborhoods or another city, geographically blocked validation can be more informative than random folds. Choose the split to match the intended prediction task.

10. Multicollinearity and heteroskedasticity

Multicollinearity means that predictors carry overlapping information. It can inflate coefficient standard errors, make coefficients unstable, and make individual effects hard to separate. Predictions within a similar predictor distribution may still be accurate. Perfect collinearity makes some coefficients unidentifiable; distribution changes and extrapolation can expose instability. Therefore “mostly harmless for prediction” is a qualified observation, not a rule.

Homoskedasticity is constant conditional error variance. A fan-shaped residual plot suggests heteroskedasticity: the uncertainty differs across observations. OLS slopes can remain unbiased under the zero-conditional-mean assumption, but conventional standard errors and prediction intervals can be wrong. Robust SEs address coefficient uncertainty under appropriate conditions; they do not repair a missing nonlinear relationship or automatically improve point predictions.

plot(fitted(m1), residuals(m1),
     xlab = "Fitted rent", ylab = "Residual")
abline(h = 0, lty = 2)
# If installed: lmtest::bptest(m1)

11. Reading a residual map

Attach predictions and errors to the same tract rows, then use a diverging color scale centered on zero. A cluster of positive errors can suggest an omitted location-related feature, a nonlinear pattern, or correlated data problems. It does not identify a causal explanation by itself.

tracts$residual <- residuals(m1)  # only if no rows were silently omitted
ggplot(tracts) +
  geom_sf(aes(fill = residual), color = NA) +
  scale_fill_gradient2(low = "#2166ac", mid = "white", high = "#b2182b",
                       midpoint = 0) +
  labs(fill = "Observed - fitted rent ($)") +
  theme_void()

Distinguish in-sample residual maps used for diagnosis from out-of-fold error maps used to inspect predictive performance. For policy triage, consider error size, survey MOE/CV, and whether the model was evaluated honestly.

Proving R Works

This reproducible numerical check uses the board’s teaching values and requires no API key. The expected RMSE is 223.61 and the predicted rent is 1,354.20.

observed <- c(500, 900)
predicted <- c(400, 1200)
round(c(
  RMSE = sqrt(mean((observed - predicted)^2)),
  predicted_rent = 690 + 0.0081 * 82000,
  residual = 1650 - (690 + 0.0081 * 82000)
), 2)
          RMSE predicted_rent       residual 
        223.61        1354.20         295.80

Check Your Understanding

  1. Actual rent is $1,800 and predicted rent is $1,500. What is the residual?
  2. A slope is 0.0081. What does $10,000 more annual income imply for predicted monthly rent?
  3. Model A has higher training \(R^2\) but higher held-out RMSE than B. Which is better supported for prediction under that evaluation?
  4. Does a tiny station-count p-value prove that adding a station raises rent?
  5. In five-fold CV with 100 records, how many observations train each model and how many are held out?
  6. Are rent_cv and cross-validation the same thing?

Answers: 1. +$300, above prediction. 2. +$81, as an association. 3. B, assuming comparable data and a meaningful difference. 4. No. 5. 80 and 20, repeated five times. 6. No: estimate uncertainty versus held-out model evaluation.

Reflection

The central habit is to specify the question before interpreting a regression. The same coefficient can help prediction while being inadequate for a causal claim. A residual is useful evidence about where a model misses, but ACS uncertainty and spatial structure also matter when interpreting that miss.

Questions I Still Have

  • How should a housing agency combine model error and ACS uncertainty when deciding which tracts need further investigation?

## Reflection

The central habit is to specify the question before interpreting a regression.
The same coefficient can help prediction while being inadequate for a causal
claim. A residual is useful evidence about where a model misses, but ACS
uncertainty and spatial structure also matter when interpreting that miss.
